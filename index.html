How it works

  1. Find your pitch. A guided test with headphones matches the pitch of your tinnitus.
  2. Notch your music. Notched cuts a one-octave gap around that pitch out of songs you already own.
  3. Listen every day. Play your notched library and track your daily listening minutes against a goal.
About the science What the research says, and what it doesn't

In short

Notched music is a promising but unproven approach. Early studies found it made tinnitus quieter; larger, better-controlled studies are mixed. It's low-risk at a moderate volume, and best used alongside advice from a doctor or audiologist.

The idea

Tonal tinnitus often sits at a pitch where hearing is damaged. One theory is that brain cells tuned to that pitch become over-active because the cells around them no longer keep them in check.

Music with a gap cut out around your tinnitus pitch keeps exercising the neighbouring cells while leaving the over-active ones quiet. Over months, that may help calm the area of the brain linked to the tinnitus. Researchers call this tailor-made notched music training (TMNMT).

What the research found

The first study Okamoto et al., PNAS, 2010

Eight people listened to their own notched music for a year. Their tinnitus got quieter, and brain scans showed less tinnitus-related activity, compared with eight people on a placebo version.

Very small, but it started the field.

Pitch matters Teismann et al., PLoS One, 2011

After five days of intensive listening, tinnitus improved only in people whose pitch was 8 kHz or lower, not in those above 8 kHz.

Small (20 people) and short.

The largest blinded trial Stein et al., BMC Neurology, 2016

100 people listened two hours a day for three months. Tinnitus loudness fell more than with placebo music, but the main measures of distress and handicap didn't improve.

Loudness improved; how much it bothered people didn't.

Review of 7 trials Tavanai et al., Eur Arch Otorhinolaryngol, 2024

Pooling the trials found no clear effect on tinnitus handicap after three or six months, though most studies that measured loudness found it improved.

Few studies to combine.

Review of 14 trials Jiang et al., Eur Arch Otorhinolaryngol, 2025

Across 793 people, notched music reduced tinnitus handicap and loudness more than ordinary music after three months.

The trials were small, and this disagrees with the 2024 review.

US clinical guideline AAO-HNSF tinnitus guideline, updated 2026

Doctors should recommend CBT for persistent, bothersome tinnitus, and may recommend sound-based therapy. It advises against routine medicines and supplements.

Covers sound therapy in general, not notched music specifically.

What this means for you

  • It seems most likely to help with how loud tinnitus sounds, and less with how much it bothers you.
  • It has worked best when tinnitus is below about 8 kHz. Music has little sound above that, so the gap matters less.
  • Studies used one to two hours a day for at least three months. Short trials showed smaller effects.
  • Talking therapy (CBT) has the strongest evidence for reducing tinnitus distress. Ask your doctor about it too.
  • Keep the volume moderate. Loud listening can damage hearing further.
Notched Music is not a medical device and doesn't diagnose or treat any condition. See a doctor or audiologist about your tinnitus, especially if it's new, in one ear only, pulsing, or changing.
